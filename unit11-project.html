Individual Project

Cloud Strategy Assessment for CareLink Health

This individual project focused on developing a complete cloud strategy for CareLink Health, a hypothetical medium-sized private healthcare provider operating several clinics across the United Kingdom.

The organisation was assumed to rely on a mixture of clinic-based servers and disconnected cloud services, creating challenges related to scalability, resilience, security, regulatory compliance and cost management.

Project Overview

The proposed solution was a phased hybrid-cloud strategy centred on Microsoft Azure, while retaining selected on-premises systems where legacy applications, medical-device dependencies or latency requirements made immediate migration unsuitable.

The project examined cloud architecture, Infrastructure as Code using Terraform, automation, artificial intelligence, hybrid-cloud integration, disaster recovery, cybersecurity and regulatory compliance.

Rather than treating cloud migration as a purely technical exercise, the project considered how technical architecture should support wider business objectives, including service availability, patient experience, cost control, security and future scalability.

Business and Technical Objectives

The cloud strategy was designed around measurable objectives rather than general improvements. The main targets proposed for CareLink Health were:

  • 99.95% availability for patient-facing services.
  • A Recovery Time Objective (RTO) of four hours.
  • A Recovery Point Objective (RPO) of 15 minutes for critical transactional data.
  • At least 80% of infrastructure changes delivered through approved IaC pipelines.
  • A target reduction of 15% in avoidable infrastructure expenditure within 18 months.

These objectives helped make the cloud transformation measurable and allowed technical decisions to be evaluated against business outcomes.

Proposed Cloud Architecture

A hybrid architecture was selected because not every healthcare workload is equally suitable for immediate public-cloud migration. Patient-facing applications, analytics and new digital services would move to Azure, while systems dependent on local medical hardware or specialist legacy software could remain within individual clinics.

Infrastructure as Code and Automation

Terraform was selected as the primary Infrastructure as Code tool. Reusable modules would define networking, application services, databases, monitoring and access controls. This allows environments to be deployed repeatedly from version-controlled configuration rather than being manually created through a graphical interface.

Infrastructure changes would follow a plan → review → approve → apply workflow. Automated security checks and policy validation would be performed before production deployment, helping to reduce configuration errors and unauthorised infrastructure changes.

Automation would also support patching, backup verification, compliance checking, monitoring and deployment. However, high-impact actions affecting clinical systems would retain human approval to reduce the risk of automation causing operational disruption.

Artificial Intelligence Integration

AI was introduced initially through lower-risk operational use cases rather than autonomous clinical decision-making.

Historical appointment volumes, cancellations, clinic capacity and seasonal patterns could be analysed using a forecasting model to predict future demand. This information could help CareLink improve staffing and appointment allocation.

A second use case involved analysing infrastructure and security telemetry for abnormal behaviour. AI-assisted monitoring could help technical teams identify unusual authentication patterns, performance degradation or resource consumption before these develop into more serious incidents.

I deliberately separated operational AI from clinical AI because the consequences of an inaccurate staffing forecast are significantly lower than the consequences of an incorrect diagnosis or treatment recommendation. Clinical AI would therefore require additional validation, governance, bias testing, auditing and human oversight.

Security and Compliance

Security was a central consideration because healthcare information is classified as special category personal data under UK GDPR.

The proposed environment followed Zero Trust principles, including multi-factor authentication, least-privilege access, role-based access control, managed identities, encryption and privileged-access reviews.

The project also considered UK healthcare-specific governance, including the Caldicott Principles, NCSC Cloud Security Principles and the NHS Data Security and Protection Toolkit where applicable.

Risk Impact Main Mitigation
Ransomware or destructive attack Critical MFA, segmentation, isolated backups and tested recovery
Cloud or IaC misconfiguration High Peer review, IaC scanning and Policy-as-Code
Regional cloud outage Critical Availability zones, replicated data and DR testing
Data breach Critical Encryption, RBAC, access reviews and central logging
Connectivity failure High Redundant connections and local degraded-mode procedures
Vendor dependency Medium Exit planning, standard formats and portable IaC
AI error or bias High if clinical Validation, monitoring and human oversight

Migration Strategy

The migration was divided into three phases to reduce operational risk.

Phase 1 – Months 0–6

Establish identity, connectivity, logging, Terraform modules, security policies, monitoring and disaster recovery standards.

Phase 2 – Months 6–12

Migrate lower-dependency workloads such as online booking, telemedicine and selected analytics services.

Phase 3 – Months 12–24

Modernise legacy clinical systems only where testing demonstrates that device integration, latency, security and continuity requirements can be maintained.

This phased strategy reduced the risk associated with attempting a large-scale cloud migration at once and allowed CareLink to retain on-premises systems where there was still a strong technical or clinical justification.

Future Recommendations

The project also considered how the proposed cloud platform could evolve beyond the initial migration.

Edge Computing

During years 1–3, edge computing could support remote patient monitoring and connected medical devices. Local gateways could process basic telemetry close to the device before transmitting relevant data to cloud services.

Privacy-Preserving Technologies

Federated learning and confidential computing could later allow collaboration between clinics without unnecessarily centralising raw patient data.

Quantum Cloud

Quantum computing was placed in the longer-term research horizon rather than the production roadmap. CareLink could monitor developments and experiment with specialised optimisation problems, but production healthcare workloads should only adopt quantum technologies when clear technical and business value can be demonstrated.

Reflection

This project helped me bring together many of the topics studied throughout the module. Instead of evaluating cloud technologies individually, I had to consider how architecture, security, automation, AI, compliance, disaster recovery and cost management interact within a single organisational strategy.

One of the main lessons I learned was that selecting cloud technology requires evaluating trade-offs. A more technically advanced service is not always the best option. For example, App Service was more appropriate than Kubernetes for the initial patient-facing applications because it provided the required scalability without unnecessary orchestration complexity.

I also developed a stronger understanding of the importance of measurable objectives. Defining availability, RTO, RPO, cost and automation targets made it possible to evaluate whether the cloud strategy actually delivered business value.

Overall, the project strengthened my ability to approach cloud computing as both a technical and strategic discipline rather than simply a collection of individual services.
